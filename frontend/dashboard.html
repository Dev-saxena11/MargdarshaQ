🧭 Problem & Approach

[ 01 ]

The problem: getting a fleet through real traffic — not just finding short routes

A delivery company has one depot, a handful of vans, and a list of customers who have each been promised a time slot. Every van has a load limit and has to be back by the end of the shift. The job is to decide which van visits which customers, in which order.

The trap is treating this as a shortest-distance puzzle. The shortest route through a city at 9am is often the slowest one to drive, because the short way is the way everyone else is also taking. What matters is time on the road through actual traffic, and whether the customer was reached inside the slot they were promised.

Formally: a capacitated vehicle routing problem with time windows (CVRPTW), priced on a congested, one-way-aware road network.
[ 02 ]

Why classical and greedy routing fails: it gets stuck in a dead end

There are too many possible plans to simply try them all — fifteen stops can be ordered more ways than there are seconds in the age of the universe. So ordinary routing software guesses: send each van to whichever stop is nearest right now. That looks sensible and it walks straight into the jam.

Worse, once it has committed to that shape of route, every small change it can try makes things look briefly worse, so it stops improving and calls it done — like always taking the first turning that looks clear, then finding yourself in a dead end you cannot back out of.

This is a local minimum: a plan that beats everything near it, while a far better plan exists somewhere the search never looked.
[ 03 ]

Our approach: a search that can jump out, then tidy up

Our method borrows an idea from quantum physics. Instead of only ever stepping to the next-best option, a plan can jump straight to a completely different arrangement on the other side of the search space — so it escapes the dead end the greedy method is stuck in and keeps finding better plans long after the greedy one has given up.

Once it has a good plan, a second pass tidies it: it re-reads its own routes for stops visited in a silly order — a crossing, a doubling back — and straightens them out.

Technique: quantum-behaved particle swarm optimisation (QPSO), refined by 2-opt / or-opt local search. The convergence curve further down shows this happening iteration by iteration.
